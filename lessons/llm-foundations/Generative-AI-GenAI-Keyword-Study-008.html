Context

Generative artificial intelligence describes AI systems that can create content, such as text, images, audio, code, or video, by learning patterns from training data.

A practical understanding covers how generation differs from classification, how prompts and training affect outputs, how to use verified source material, and how to manage accuracy, safety, and cost.

1. What does generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) do, and how is it different from a model that classifies information?

Correct: C. Generative AI produces content such as text, images, audio, code, and video from learned patterns. A classifier usually assigns labels to existing inputs, such as spam or not spam. Both use learned models, but their immediate outputs differ. Generative systems can still make mistakes, repeat patterns, or create content that needs review.

2. Why can a GenAI model create different outputs from similar prompts instead of copying one stored answer?

Correct: A. GenAI models learn relationships in training examples and use those learned patterns to generate outputs. Prompts, context, and generation settings can influence the result, so similar requests may produce different content. The model does not simply choose from a fixed answer list, although memorization or copying can sometimes occur and should be checked.

3. What happens when a user sends a prompt to a trained GenAI model to generate content?

Correct: D. A GenAI application prepares the user's prompt and any supporting context, sends it to an already trained model, and receives generated output. The application may check, format, or filter the result before showing it. This is inference, not full retraining. Generation follows the model's architecture and configuration, which differ across text and image systems.

4. How do a prompt, training data, and model parameters play different roles in GenAI output?

Correct: B. Training data provides examples from which the model learns patterns. Training updates numerical parameters, often called weights, that shape later predictions. A prompt supplies a current instruction or context during inference. Sending a prompt does not normally change those weights. Even a precise prompt can still lead to unsupported or inaccurate output.

5. How could a marketing team use GenAI to draft an advertisement without losing control of its brand information?

Correct: C. A marketing team can supply approved product details, audience, tone, and campaign constraints to guide a model's draft. The team should then check product claims, trademarks, style, and required disclosures before publishing. GenAI is useful for ideation and first drafts; it does not replace product truth, legal review, or editorial judgment.

6. Why should teams check GenAI outputs for hallucinations, privacy risks, and misleading media?

Correct: A. A model may produce false statements that sound convincing, reveal sensitive information supplied in a prompt, or create media that people mistake for real evidence. Teams should limit unnecessary data exposure, check factual claims, and clearly handle synthetic content. Safety review and user permissions are part of deployment, not optional decoration.

7. A model creates a beautiful product image, but the packaging label is wrong. What should the team do?

Correct: D. Image generators can create convincing scenes without reproducing a real product's label or shape accurately. The team should start from approved product photos or references, use tools and workflows that preserve required details, and review the result closely. Attractive appearance alone does not guarantee a faithful representation of the advertised product.

8. You are asked to launch a GenAI assistant that creates customer emails and marketing images. How would you balance quality, safety, speed, and cost?

Correct: B. Begin with clear use cases, approved data, and the smallest capable model or service for each task. Draft text and images through separate, controlled workflows. Validate factual claims, privacy, image accuracy, and user permissions before release. Measure useful quality, response time, cost per output, and failure rates; add human review where needed.
